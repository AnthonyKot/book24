# 5. Deal with crosses and corners

*What can I claim, and what must the defenders cover?*

## The situation

A composite, not a goal you reported: a corner comes from your left. You can see the ball rising, but a defender and an attacker are between you and the place where it appears to be dropping. You take a step, hesitate, and the attacker makes the first contact while you are between your starting position and the ball. Staying would have left a possible header to save; going would have required a route and a controlled claim. Which was available? Your remembered flank-to-middle goals do not answer this: a pass from the flank is not necessarily an airborne cross. This chapter opens a new situation without rewriting those matches.

## What you can change and what you cannot

You can watch the delivery, judge your route and tell teammates when you are taking responsibility for the ball. You cannot remove every opponent from the penalty area, guarantee the defender wins a header, or make a poor view into a clear one by shouting louder.

You have clarified difficulty with sideways diving. That tells us nothing definite about jumping upward, catching overhead or landing after a jump. This chapter therefore begins with standing claims, not a sequence of high leaps. Your height does not define a circle of balls you must collect, and your weight does not establish which claims you cannot make.

The defence shares the situation. If you stay available for the finish, somebody still needs to deal with the delivery and its receiver. If you claim, teammates need to understand your call. A keeper's shout does not make the flight easier or guarantee that an opponent will stop competing.

## The decision: can you meet this ball with control?

**As the delivery travels, judge the flight and the route: can you reach the ball in time using a collection you can control?** If you judge that you can, commit to the claim and call clearly. If you cannot, keep adjusting for the possible finish and leave the delivery to the defender responsible for it. This is the book's beginner working rule for a catch attempt, not a sourced distance threshold or a complete account of every way to defend a cross.

Going on the strength of the word “corner” can put you underneath a ball you cannot reach. Staying automatically can leave an uncontested ball unclaimed. Between those errors is a judgement about this delivery, from this starting position, with these people in the way. The ball keeps moving while you decide.

FIFA's high-ball fundamentals identify speed, path and height as information for meeting the ball at the right moment. Its practices also change the destination across the goal and require the keeper to adjust their footwork. We retain those ideas without copying the session's jumping and punching work.

### Flight comes before a promise to collect

Before a corner is taken, you can see the delivery side and where players are standing. You cannot yet see the actual flight. Prepare to read it rather than promising yourself that the next ball is yours.

Once it is delivered, follow whether it is arriving quickly, hanging, passing over you or travelling towards another receiving place. A slow ball can still be beyond your reach; a nearby ball can arrive before your hands can meet it.

Do not use the painted small penalty area as the answer. A ball inside it can be screened or too quick to claim; a ball beyond it may be reachable. Practice markers organise deliveries, not responsibility in a match.

### The route includes other people

An empty-pitch catch shows what you can do without obstruction. In a crowded area, ask where your first steps would take you. If the route depends on moving through a teammate or an opponent, it is different from the route you rehearsed alone.

Nearby players do not make every claim impossible, but an unobstructed catch does not establish your ability to collect through a crowd. Our first squad form adds a visible defender and an agreed route, not somebody challenging you in the air.

Your starting place matters for both options. From it you need a view of the delivery and a possible response to the finish. There is no fixed number of metres from the line that settles that tradeoff for every corner. In the rehearsal, check the view with a teammate and alter the starting place if you repeatedly have to step around them. You are testing an arrangement, not discovering a compulsory spot.

### The call describes your action

The FA's out-of-possession session links goalkeeper communication to defending crosses and uses “keeper's” for a ball the keeper can claim. Agree a familiar call with your team before practising. Here we use **“keeper”** for your claim and **“away”** when the defender is to deal with the delivery. “Away” is the book's suggested team agreement, not a quotation from that session.

Say “keeper” when you have made the judgement and are going, rather than using it to ask whether someone thinks you should go. Your teammate needs time to respond. In our practice they leave your agreed route clear; in a match they must also respond to the opponents around them. The call is coordination, not permission to collide or an instruction for everybody to stop defending.

If the flight changes your judgement after you call, communicate the change. Pretending the original promise is still achievable will not improve the collection. The practice pauses after such a change so you can identify what you misread. It does not reward completing a bad decision just to appear decisive.

### Staying still leaves a job to do

“Away” does not finish your involvement. Follow the ball and the player who may contact it, adjust to the new threat and prepare for the finish. A short corner also moves the situation before a cross has even been delivered: follow the actual ball rather than guarding the place where the corner taker began.

The defender's clearance can succeed while you remain prepared; that is a successful shared action. If their first contact comes back into play, the held-or-live question from chapter 3 still applies to your next save. We are not adding a second decision to this chapter's practice. Its central choice remains your claim or the defender's delivery to deal with.

Some balls require a punch rather than a catch. FIFA teaches that as its own technical work. This chapter does not tell you to stay merely because a catch is impossible when you already have a useful punching action, nor does it teach an improvised punch as the remedy for hesitation. Our beginner practice isolates controlled catches and deferring to a teammate; contested catches and punching need further instruction.

## The practice: read the delivery, claim or prepare

This is one practice, adapted into solo, pair and squad forms. It develops flight judgement and a clear action at a level where jumping is unnecessary. The setup, repetition counts, calls and progression limits below are the book's suggestions. They have not been tested with you.

Use one ball and a few markers on clear ground. Keep the collection area away from posts and other obstacles. Warm up with easy movement and handling. Deliveries should be gentle enough to collect with both feet on the ground, without leaning backwards or stretching into a fall. An overhit ball is a reset, not a challenge to chase it.

### Alone: rehearse a claim you can already control

Mark a starting place and a nearby receiving place, about one comfortable walking step ahead and to one side. Hold the ball, make a small gentle toss towards that place, follow it, move and catch within comfortable standing reach. Retain the ball through the end of the action, then return to the start. Do three repetitions on each side, pausing between them.

Good means you watch the ball rather than your marker, use the actual flight to adjust the step, and finish balanced with possession. If you have to lunge, reduce the toss or the separation. You need not put the ball above your head; the point is watching a flight and moving into a collection you control.

Then do three walking rehearsals on each side without tossing. Imagine a delivery outside that comfortable collection and rehearse “away”, adjusting to face the imagined receiving place instead of walking out for a claim. This is a rehearsal of the other action, not a test of your judgement. You know what you tossed, and an imagined flight supplies no surprise. Solo work cannot establish which real corners you should claim.

### With one teammate: let someone else supply the flight

Put your starting marker in front of the goal, or use two markers as a goal on open ground. Your teammate stands about four metres in front and to one side. This is a convenient serving distance, not a claim threshold. They use gentle two-hand tosses, not kicked crosses.

Begin with six deliveries, three from each side, to a place you can reach in comfortable steps and catch while standing. Watch the flight, move, call “keeper” as you commit and retain the ball at the finish. Agree the general receiving area beforehand; the teammate should not surprise you with a high or driven ball.

Next agree a second receiving place, clearly farther to the side than you can reach before that gentle toss arrives. Keep it on clear ground and away from the posts. Your teammate alternates between the two destinations for six more deliveries. Read the flight rather than asking which one is coming. For the reachable ball, claim. For the other, call “away” and adjust for a possible finish from its destination. Let it land; retrieve it after the repetition has stopped.

Good is a judgement that produces a clear action, without beginning a chase and then abandoning it halfway. A call without movement does not count as a claim; running out without controlling the ball does not count as a successful catch. If the difference between destinations is unclear, simplify the serves before judging yourself.

There is no defender in this form. The “away” call has no receiver, and there is no real finish to save. It rehearses your choice, not a complete defence of the delivery. Do not invent a dangerous late shot to make it more realistic.

### With the squad: give the defender a real task

Use you, one server and one defender, with a ball and the same two receiving places. Start with the defender able to see the server and outside your agreed claim route. Explain both calls before the first delivery: “keeper” means you collect; “away” means the defender deals with the ball while you prepare for what could follow.

The server gently tosses either to the comfortable claim place or towards the defender's receiving place. On “keeper”, the defender leaves your route clear and you collect. On “away”, they let the ball drop and control or clear it with a foot into empty space. No headers, jump, contest or shot follows. Stop and reset after the first action. Do four repetitions from each serving side, with a pause between them.

Good means the ball and the two players' actions agree: your claim is controlled, or the defender deals with the delivery while you remain prepared. Ask the defender whether your call arrived in time for them to act. That is practice feedback.

If those eight repetitions are orderly, move the server towards a corner on the same side and shorten the toss as needed to retain standing catches. Do another four from each side only if the delivery stays manageable. Changing the angle is enough; do not also add an attacker and increase the height. If a teammate cannot serve a learnable ball from there, retain the earlier position. A hand toss from a corner is still a simplified delivery, not a regulation corner kick.

The FA session supplies the shared defending situation; our version removes the attackers and scoring. FIFA's high-ball work supplies changing destinations; our version removes the jump. Later aerial instruction needs someone who can check your take-off, catch and landing. A record of standing catches cannot clear that progression for you.

## Where it stops

The practice leaves out curved or hard deliveries, wind, screens and competition. Its serves are deliberately easy to distinguish. A match compresses those differences and may leave a genuine uncertainty. Use the rehearsal to make your action clearer, not to assign yourself a permanent claim area.

Neither “keeper” nor “away” repairs missing marking. Before a match, agree with the defender who deals with a delivery you leave and who follows the receiver; the whole corner arrangement belongs to the team. Chapter 6 develops that organisation. This chapter does not choose a complete marking system or demand a teammate on each post.

## The observation: who dealt with the first ball?

After the next match, record one thing for each airborne cross or corner you remember: **who dealt with the delivery at its first contact?** Use `keeper`, `defender`, `opponent`, `untouched`, or `unsure`. A line can read `corner from left — defender`. These are recording categories, not scores for good and bad decisions.

Keeper means you made the first contact, whether you caught or punched; it does not certify success. Defender means a teammate reached it first. Opponent means an attacker did. Untouched means it passed through without contact; unsure means you do not remember. Include deliveries that caused no shot. Keep chapter 2's ordinary flank-to-middle passes in their existing category rather than counting them again as high crosses.

A run of opponent entries gives you and a defender situations to review together: was the claim available, was somebody responsible for the receiver, or is the flight still missing from memory? It cannot prove you should have come for all of them. Defender entries may show the shared arrangement working; keeper entries still leave control and the route open for review. Record the first contact you remember, and leave the judgement for evidence you actually have.
