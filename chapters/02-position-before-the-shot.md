# 2. Position before the shot

*Where should I stand as the ball moves, and when should I be ready?*

## The situation

A composite example of the pattern you described, not a reconstruction of a particular goal: the ball is on your right, an attacker passes it into the middle, and another attacker shoots. You were protecting the goal against the player on the flank. Now the finish comes from somewhere else. Perhaps you stayed in the first position; perhaps you moved but were still travelling when the shot came. Neither was recorded, so neither is a diagnosis. In [your account of two matches](../matches/two-match-patterns.md), flank, pass into the middle, accurate finish made up roughly three goals in ten, an estimate rather than a count. This chapter asks what happens between the pass and the shot. You clarified that sideways diving is difficult for you. The first question is whether your position and balance gave you a chance to respond before any dive was needed.

## What you can change and what you cannot

An unmarked receiver is a problem the defence shares with you. You cannot reposition every teammate while following a pass. A well-placed finish can also beat a keeper who is prepared. Neither becomes a keeper's mistake simply because the ball went in.

Your part is the transition between the two threats. Your position for the player on the flank is a starting point, not a place to remain after the ball has moved. Reaching another position is only part of the job: you also need to be able to respond from it. The review therefore separates three possibilities: you did not adjust, you adjusted but arrived unbalanced, or you were prepared and the finish beat you. Where you cannot remember, leave it unknown.

The working distinction is between **being set** and being stuck. Here, set means balanced and ready to act; it does not mean planting your feet and refusing to move. A receiver who takes another touch can change the shooting position again. The chapter's decision is when to finish travelling and prepare for that next action.

## The decision: move with the pass, prepare for the finish

**The working cue is the pass leaving the wide player's foot: update your position while it travels, and prepare for a possible shot as it reaches the receiver.** If the receiver carries the ball instead, adjust again. This is the book's beginner working rule, not a guarantee that you will reach an ideal position before every shot.

FIFA's transition analysis illustrates a keeper repositioning as a pass crosses the attack, preparing for a first-time finish, and then adjusting again to a receiving touch. The useful sequence is changing position, preparing, and responding; the professional's speed and starting depth are not prescriptions for you.

### The position belongs to the ball's new location

Picture the example at walking pace. The wide player has the ball. Look from that ball towards the two posts: this is the goal they can shoot at. Now put the ball at the central receiver's feet and look again. The view is different even though the posts have not moved. Your position must be checked against this second view.

You can inspect that change without any shooting. Ask a teammate to stand with the ball first wide and then centrally. From each place, have them describe what they see on either side of you. Their view is a practice aid, not an exact model of your reach. A player sees openings that a keeper may cover after the shot, and a slow inspection leaves out the pressure of a match. It still makes one error visible: protecting the old shooting angle after the pass has changed it.

![Two overhead schematic panels. A wide ball has one shooting angle; after a pass into the middle, the receiver has a different angle. The goalkeeper adjusts while the ball travels, then prepares for the finish. Positions are illustrative, not prescribed distances.](img/02-changing-angle.svg)

The diagram supplies no compulsory spot to stand. Stepping forward changes the shooting angle but also changes the space behind you and the time available to react. Remaining deeper has a different tradeoff. There is no line on the pitch that settles all three. Start the practice at a depth you can manage, check the view with your teammate, and change one thing at a time. This chapter does not require you to sprint out towards the receiver.

### Travel without losing the next action

In FIFA's penalty-area session, keepers adjust to deliveries from different positions; its coaching points emphasise following the ball and using short steps to change angle. The beginner adaptation here removes the dives and contested collections and keeps the change of position..

Use the slow repetitions below to find steps that leave you able to stop or change direction. Do not turn the practice into racing sideways and hoping your last step lands in time. Equally, tiny steps that never get you across are not the goal. Let the teammate slow the pass so you can practise the whole sequence before making it harder.

Your eyes need the ball's path and the receiver's action. In the exercise, the receiver is known and there are no bodies screening the pass. That makes this a simpler task than the match. A pass you could not see and a pass you saw but followed late are different problems; note the distinction rather than assuming both need quicker feet.

### Prepare before discovering the shot's destination

The receiver may finish first time. Waiting to see whether the shot is going left or right before preparing would leave the preparation until after contact. The practice therefore asks you to be ready for either outcome as the ball arrives, without choosing a save in advance.

FIFA's goal-defending session includes both first-time finishes and finishes after a touch, with a balanced low preparation for the shot. Its distances and diving demands remain outside this beginner practice..

A comfortable ready position here is one from which you can take the next step without first untangling your feet. Let your teammate check whether you are still drifting when they make contact. Preparing does not require a jump, a deep squat, or a fall to the ground. It is also not a promise to be motionless through the entire encounter. If the receiver takes a touch across the goal, the picture changes and you follow it.

This is different from chapter 4's decision to collect a loose ball. In this chapter, the receiver keeps possession and you prepare for a finish. If the ball genuinely becomes available to win, that is the breakaway chapter's question. Do not add an automatic slide to a practice about readiness.

## The practice: change the picture, then receive

The one practice follows the same sequence in all three forms: a wide threat changes to a central threat, you adjust, then prepare. The setups and repetition counts are the book's suggested starting doses, not numbers taken from a coaching session. Keep the surface even and the pace comfortable. There are no required dives, slides, jumps or collisions.

**Alone.** Use a goal or two markers representing its posts, with one marker for a wide ball and another for a central receiver. Allow enough room to walk between your two keeper positions without crowding the markers. Inspect each angle first; choose the positions as references for this exercise, not match-day laws.

Start facing the wide marker. Imagine the pass leaving it, adjust towards your position for the central marker, then settle facing that marker. Check whether you could take a step in either direction without a preparatory shuffle. Walk back and repeat five times from each side, with a pause between repetitions. Good is arriving balanced with your attention on the new ball location. There is no live pass or shot, so the solo version rehearses the movement sequence, not reading an attacker.

**With one teammate.** Use a ball, a goal and a wide and central marker. Your teammate starts at the wide marker with the ball. For the first five repetitions, they carry it slowly towards the central marker; you adjust as its position changes. Once there, they pause and send you a gentle ground pass. Repeat from the other side. They aim within your comfortable standing reach, and you let a misplaced pass go rather than adding a dive.

This is deliberately slower than flank-to-middle football. One teammate cannot be both the passer and a separate receiver, so carrying the ball introduces extra time. It is useful for checking your movement and preparation, not for proving you can handle a first-time finish.

For the next five repetitions from each side, your teammate varies whether they make the gentle delivery on arriving at the marker or take another controlled touch first. Adjust to that touch when necessary. They need not wait until you look ready, but the delivery stays gentle and within standing reach. If every repetition catches you travelling, slow the movement rather than making you chase harder. Good is recognising the extra touch and being able to respond at contact; a ball collected after stumbling is not evidence that the preparation worked.

**With the squad.** Use two teammates, a ball and a goal: one wide passer and one central receiver. The receiver stands at a distance from which they can send an accurate, gentle ground delivery to you; choose it together rather than copying an elite drill's distance. The passer rolls the ball centrally, the receiver controls it and sends it towards you, and you adjust during the pass and prepare for the finish. Reset after every ball, with no rebounds or challenges.

Do five repetitions from each flank. If the passes and your movement are controlled, do another five from each side with the receiver choosing between a first-time gentle delivery and one touch before delivering. Keep the delivery within standing reach. A first-time miss is a reason to reset, not permission for the receiver to run in and finish the rebound. Good is a keeper who follows the actual ball and is balanced for contact, rather than memorising a route and falling into it.

Ask the receiver for one piece of feedback: were you prepared when they delivered, still travelling, or was it unclear? No score for how spectacular the collection looked. The squad form retains the wide-to-central change and receiving options from the FIFA sessions, with their speed, diving and continued rebound play removed. If only one teammate is available, use the pair form and accept its limitation.

## Where it stops

Controlled ground deliveries leave out screens, deflections, aerial crosses, hard shots and pressure from several attackers. Being balanced in this practice will not prove that your match positioning is correct. It gives you a repeatable way to investigate the moment before contact.

You may also have too little time to finish repositioning before a first-time shot. That is a limit to record, not proof of laziness or an instruction to freeze in the old position. The present practice slows the situation enough to learn it; the match may expose a different need. Chapter 3 adds the saving and recovery technique, and chapter 5 addresses airborne deliveries. Neither is assumed here.

## The observation: prepared, travelling, or unsure

For each flank-to-middle finish you remember from the next match, record one observation: **at the receiver's shooting contact, was I prepared, still travelling, or unsure?** Include saves and shots that miss, not just goals. A line can read `flank to middle — travelling`, without an exact minute or a verdict about blame.

Prepared means you remember being able to respond from a balanced position; it does not certify that you chose the best angle. Travelling means you remember still completing the move as the shot came. Unsure means the moment is missing from your memory. Do not infer the answer from where the shot finished.

Repeated travelling entries give the practice a question to investigate: did your adjustment begin late, was the route too large, or was the delivery too quick for the available time? Repeated unsure entries mean you need clearer observation, perhaps a teammate's account. Prepared entries followed by goals leave the angle, the save and the quality of the finish open. The next match's observation remains just those three choices: **prepared, travelling, or unsure at shooting contact.**
